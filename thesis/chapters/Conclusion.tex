\chapter{Conclusion} \label{ch:conclusion}

In this thesis, we studied randomized methods for overdetermined least-squares
problems and their behavior in mixed-precision arithmetic. The main point is that
randomized approximation and reduced precision should not be treated as two
independent sources of computational improvement. In least-squares problems,
both act on the same operator, and both are filtered through conditioning.

From the theoretical side, the thesis shows why sketching methods are useful.
Their role is not only to reduce dimension, but to preserve enough of the
least-squares geometry for the reduced problem or preconditioned problem to
remain meaningful. From the numerical side, the thesis shows that the usefulness
of lower precision depends on stability. The same arithmetic choice may be safe
for one conditioning regime and unacceptable for another.

The experiments and the application chapter are meant to make this picture more
concrete. They ask where the methods are reliable, where they are computationally
advantageous, and where their failure modes begin to dominate the possible gain.
For this reason, the thesis does not aim at one universal recommendation. Its goal
is rather to give a clearer description of the tradeoff between accuracy,
conditioning, precision, and computational cost.

Natural directions for later work include a more detailed error analysis for
particular sketch families in mixed precision, more systematic comparisons of
reduced-precision implementations, and a broader study of realistic application
pipelines. At the present stage, however, the central conclusion is already clear:
randomized numerical linear algebra and mixed precision are most informative
when they are analyzed together.
