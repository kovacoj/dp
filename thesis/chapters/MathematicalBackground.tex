\chapter{Mathematical Background} \label{ch:background}

This chapter collects the mathematical material that underlies the later discussion of randomized least-squares methods. At a high level, the relevant ideas come from three sources: classical least-squares theory, geometric properties of random projections, and perturbation questions related to conditioning. Although everything takes place in finite-dimensional spaces, it is often convenient to speak in the language of operators, ranges, and subspaces rather than only in coordinates.

For an overdetermined system \(A \in \R^{n \times d}\) with \(n \ge d\), the least-squares problem
\[
    \min_{x \in \R^d} \|Ax-b\|_2
\]
asks for the vector whose image under the linear map represented by \(A\) is closest to \(b\) in Euclidean norm. Geometrically, one is projecting \(b\) onto the range of \(A\). When \(A\) has full column rank, the problem has a unique minimizer. In exact arithmetic, this minimizer can be described through the normal equations or, more stably, through an orthogonal factorization.

The randomized viewpoint replaces the original problem by a smaller one. A sketching matrix \(S \in \R^{s \times n}\), with \(s\) much smaller than \(n\), is chosen so that the geometry of the range of \(A\) is approximately preserved after compression. One then solves
\[
    \min_{x \in \R^d} \|SAx-Sb\|_2.
\]
The quality of this replacement depends on how well the sketch preserves norms and angles on the relevant subspace. This is the point at which subspace embeddings and Johnson--Lindenstrauss type phenomena enter the picture: the sketch should act almost isometrically on the subspace that carries the least-squares geometry.

Two sketch families are particularly important in this thesis. The first is the Gaussian sketch, whose entries are independent random variables with appropriate scaling. The second is the class of structured sketches, especially transforms based on randomized Hadamard matrices. Gaussian sketches are easy to analyze and often serve as a conceptual baseline, while structured sketches are computationally attractive because they exploit fast transforms.

Conditioning plays a central role throughout. Even when sketching gives a good approximation in principle, the numerical behavior of the reduced problem depends strongly on the conditioning of \(SA\), that is, on the stability of the compressed operator restricted to the relevant subspace. This becomes even more important once finite-precision arithmetic is taken into account, since rounding perturbations may be harmless for a well-conditioned problem and severe for an ill-conditioned one.

The later mixed-precision discussion therefore rests on a simple principle: randomized approximation error and floating-point error should not be studied in isolation. A useful theory must account for both sources of perturbation at once.
