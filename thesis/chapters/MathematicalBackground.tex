\chapter{Mathematical Background} \label{ch:background}

This chapter collects the mathematical material that underlies the later discussion of randomized least-squares methods. At a high level, the relevant ideas come from three sources: classical least-squares theory, geometric properties of random projections, and perturbation questions related to conditioning.

For an overdetermined system \(A \in \R^{n \times d}\) with \(n \ge d\), the least-squares problem
\[
    \min_{x \in \R^d} \|Ax-b\|_2
\]
asks for the vector whose image under \(A\) is closest to \(b\) in Euclidean norm. When \(A\) has full column rank, the problem has a unique minimizer. In exact arithmetic, this minimizer can be described through the normal equations or, more stably, through orthogonal factorization.

The randomized viewpoint replaces the original problem by a smaller one. A sketching matrix \(S \in \R^{s \times n}\), with \(s\) much smaller than \(n\), is chosen so that the geometry of the column space of \(A\) is approximately preserved. One then solves
\[
    \min_{x \in \R^d} \|SAx-Sb\|_2.
\]
The quality of this replacement depends on how well the sketch preserves norms and angles on the relevant subspace. This is the point at which subspace embeddings and Johnson--Lindenstrauss type phenomena enter the picture.

Two sketch families are particularly important in this thesis. The first is the Gaussian sketch, whose entries are independent random variables with appropriate scaling. The second is the class of structured sketches, especially transforms based on randomized Hadamard matrices. Gaussian sketches are easy to analyze and often serve as a conceptual baseline, while structured sketches are computationally attractive because they exploit fast transforms.

Conditioning plays a central role throughout. Even when sketching gives a good approximation in principle, the numerical behavior of the sketched problem depends strongly on the conditioning of \(SA\). This becomes even more important once finite-precision arithmetic is taken into account, since rounding errors may be harmless for a well-conditioned system and severe for an ill-conditioned one.

The later mixed-precision discussion therefore rests on a simple principle: randomized approximation error and floating-point error should not be studied in isolation. A useful theory must account for both sources of perturbation at once.
